Omics-based approaches for the systematic profiling of mitochondrial biology

Summary
Mitochondria are essential for cellular functions such as metabolism and apoptosis. They dynamically adapt to the changing environmental demands by adjusting their protein, nucleic acid, metabolite, and lipid contents. In addition, the mitochondrial components are modulated on different levels in response to changes, including abundance, activity, and interaction. A wide range of omics-based approaches has been developed to be able to explore mitochondrial adaptation and how mitochondrial function is compromised in disease contexts. Here, we provide an overview of the omics methods that allow us to systematically investigate the different aspects of mitochondrial biology. In addition, we show examples of how these methods have provided new biological insights. The emerging use of these toolboxes provides a more comprehensive understanding of the processes underlying mitochondrial function.

Introduction
Mitochondria contribute to the maintenance of cellular and organismal health. Numerous important metabolic processes are located in the mitochondria, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, urea cycle, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), β-oxidation, and the formation of heme and iron-sulfur clusters.1,2,3 In addition, mitochondria are involved in cellular networks that maintain calcium homeostasis, apoptosis, and lipid biosynthesis.1,4,5 Consequently, in view of these fundamental cellular roles, improper regulation of mitochondrial functions is associated with a plethora of diseases. These include “primary” mitochondrial diseases (PMDs), which are caused by mutations in genes encoding for mitochondrial proteins, and “secondary” diseases, such as neurodegeneration, metabolic disorders, and heart failure, where mitochondrial dysfunction cannot be directly linked to mitochondria-associated genetic mutations.6 Environmental conditions and genetic mutations may affect various aspects of mitochondrial function that are often collectively referred to as “mitochondrial dysfunction.” This term encompasses multiple mitochondrial malfunctions, including, but not restricted to, the aberrant production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), improper mitochondrial dynamics, defective protein homeostasis, and impaired mitochondrial turnover.6
Owing to the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria, mitochondrial biology combines both local regulatory pathways inside mitochondria with cross-compartment communication to orchestrate functional responses to the changing environmental conditions.7 Developing toolboxes of cellular and molecular biology approaches to systematically interrogate how different kinds of triggers influence mitochondrial processes becomes increasingly important to gain a comprehensive understanding of how mitochondria are connected to diseases. Recent technological advances in omics-based approaches provide a number of such novel strategies to globally profile the cellular landscape of different biomolecules. In this review, we highlight how different omics methods can be applied to study multifaceted aspects of mitochondrial biology that are dynamically regulated to adjust mitochondrial capacity to cellular needs (Figure 1). Although some of the highlighted techniques can be performed on whole-cell lysates, other applications require mitochondrial purification, typically by means of differential centrifugation or affinity purification (Figure 2A). We discuss how genomics and transcriptomics are used to explore disease-related aberrations of mitochondrial genes in the nuclear and mitochondrial genome, and how mitochondrial function is adapted on the transcriptional level in different biological contexts. We then introduce how assays based on ribosome profiling and mass spectrometric translatomics can be applied to reveal various aspects of translation and import of mitochondrial proteins. Furthermore, we give an overview of recent developments harnessing the power of mass spectrometry (MS) for profiling post-translational modifications (PTMs) and complex formation of mitochondrial proteins. We address how mitochondrial research benefited from the recent advances in spatial proteomics and describe how the N-terminomics profiling can globally define the substrate spectra of mitochondrial proteases and introduce mitochondrial metabolomics. In the long term, the combination of such systematic studies in multi-omics setups will provide a comprehensive picture of mitochondrial biology to better understand the dynamic nature of mitochondrial adaptation to changing physiological and disease-related conditions.

Figure 1. Omics-based strategies to study different aspects of mitochondrial biology

Different omics-based approaches are available to monitor a variety of mitochondrial processes. Genomics can be used to identify mutations and variations in both mtDNA and nuclear DNA encoding mitochondrial genes. Dynamic adaptations of the mitochondrial transcriptome profile can be monitored by transcriptomics. Ribosome profiling and mePROD can be used to quantify translation of mitochondrial proteins in both the cytosol and mitochondria. Mitochondrial protein import can be monitored by mePRODmt, a modified version of mePROD. Post-translational modification (PTM) of mitochondrial proteins can be analyzed by PTM profiling. Many mitochondrial proteins form functional complexes or even supercomplexes, such as the OXPHOS machinery. The composition and dynamic adaption of such complexes can be characterized by complexome profiling. Changes in mitochondrial metabolism are assessed by metabolomics. Finally, N-terminomics monitor the substrates of mitochondrial proteases involved in proteostasis maintenance.

Figure 2. Technical overview of different omics approaches used to study mitochondrial biology

(A) Depending on the omics approach, both whole cell and purified mitochondria can be used as optimal input samples. Technologies including genomics, transcriptomics of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial genes, APEX-seq, ribosome profiling, and pulsed-SILAC (mePROD) for translatomics and N-terminomics can be performed on whole-cell lysates. Other techniques, such as pulsed-SILAC (mePRODmt) for importomics and metabolomics, require preceding mitochondrial purification, typically achieved by differential centrifugation or affinity purification upon cell homogenization. However, the use of purified mitochondria might also be superior for other approaches, including transcriptomics of mitochondrial genes encoded in the mitochondrial genome, complexome profiling, spatial proteomics by proximity labeling (PL), and post-translational modification (PTM) profiling to reduce background caused by non-mitochondrial RNAs and proteins.
(B) Nucleic acid-based omics approaches. Both nuclear or mitochondrial DNA can be used as input for genomic analysis. The DNA is randomly fragmented using mechanical or enzymatic approaches to produce short DNA fragments. Transcriptomic analysis can be performed on different pools of RNA depending on the experimental aim. RNA fragmentation can be achieved by applying divalent cation containing solutions, alkaline solutions, or enzymes such as RNase. For ribosome profiling, isolated polysomes are digested with ribonuclease to obtain short, ribosome-protected RNA fragments corresponding to ribosome footprints. For APEX-seq, a protein of interest is tagged with the peroxidase APEX, which covalently labels adjacent RNA molecules with biotin. Following fragmentation, biotinylated RNA is extracted by affinity purification with streptavidin. RNA fragments derived from transcriptomics, ribosome profiling, or APEX-seq are reverse transcribed to cDNA. DNA or cDNA fragments within the size range of 200–500 bp are most commonly used to prepare the library for next-generation sequencing.
(C) Protein-based omics approaches. Pulse labeling with heavy amino acid isotopes for several minutes to hours is carried out to monitor newly translated proteins with mePROD. A booster channel consisting of fully heavy labeled peptides is included in the sample setup to enhance the identification of the heavy labeled peptide fraction in the sample. During complexome profiling, protein complexes are separated under native conditions to preserve assembled complexes. Native-PAGE is the most popular option, as it requires only low sample amounts. However, other techniques, such as size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), can be superior when separating large complexes. For spatial proteomics using BioID, a protein of interest is tagged with the biotin ligase BioID, which biotinylates adjacent proteins. Due to its high affinity for biotin, streptavidin is commonly used to isolate biotinylated proteins from the extract. In N-terminomics, samples with and without protease activity are compared to identify neoN termini derived from proteolytic cleavage of the protease of interest. N-terminal labeling or blocking before tryptic digestion is an important step to remove tryptic N-terminal peptides. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are dynamic and mostly occur at low stoichiometry, which makes enrichment of PTM containing peptides an essential step during PTM profiling.
(D) Metabolomics monitors metabolites in various biological samples from different species, including biofluids, tissues, and cells. Quenching of the enzymatic activity is critical for preserving the metabolic profile. On sample homogenization and extraction, metabolites are separated by different kinds of chromatography-based techniques, the choice of which will influence the detected metabolites in the following nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) or mass spectrometry (MS) analysis.
High-throughput sequencing-based techniques to examine genomic loci and transcription of mitochondrial proteins
Genomics
Mitochondria contain their own DNA (mtDNA) that, in humans, encodes for 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs, and 13 proteins of the OXPHOS machinery.6 The other 1,000 mitochondrial proteins are encoded in the nuclear genome.8,9 Thus, mitochondrial proteins are translated in both the cytosol and the mitochondria in a tightly regulated process. Expression changes of mitochondrial genes encoded in the nucleus to adapt to fluctuating cellular conditions will affect the gene expression within the mitochondria and vice versa.10,11
Mutations in both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes can cause PMDs.12 Therein, the sequencing of mtDNA is of particular interest because mtDNA in humans harbors an approximately 10-fold higher mutation rate than nuclear DNA, owing to the lack of repair mechanisms maintaining mitochondrial genome integrity.12,13 Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has been used extensively to sequence both nuclear genes encoding for mitochondrial proteins and the entire mitochondrial genome. This technique provides high-accuracy sequencing at a relatively low cost using short reads.14 Generally, the genomic sample is fragmented, amplified, and then sequenced in parallel in a high-throughput fashion (Figure 2B). However, the short reads used for NGS come with limitations, in particular for reliable mapping in highly repetitive sites. This is especially an issue for the mitochondrial genome across vertebrates, where the repetitive sequences are widespread.15 Recent developments to overcome these limitations resulted in the establishment of third-generation sequencing technologies with which long reads of more than 10 kb can be generated, enabling sequencing of the entire mitochondrial genome in one single read.14 However, long-read sequencing is less accurate, and further optimization of data processing will be required for broader use in the future.
Mutations in the mitochondrial genome occur sporadically and, due to the high copy number of mtDNA, can cause heteroplasmy, where both wildtype and mutant mtDNA copies co-exist in the same cell.16 Assessing mitochondrial heteroplasmy is clinically important, since the severity of PMDs often correlates with the level of heteroplasmy.12,16 Thus, the heteroplasmic percentage should be considered an additional factor for PMD diagnosis to avoid misinterpretation of causative effects of mtDNA mutations.14 Mitochondrial whole genome sequencing is a powerful tool for this purpose, since it can be performed without additional amplification of the sample material due to the naturally high copy number of mtDNA.14 An additional recent development in long-read whole genome sequencing of mtDNA further accelerates this technique by utilizing Cas9 to introduce barcodes, which allow multiplexing of several sequencing samples in one run.17
Transcriptomics
RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), an NGS-based approach to quantify global transcripts, has become a powerful tool to resolve mitochondrial gene expression changes in both physiological and pathological contexts. Briefly, purified RNA is reverse transcribed to cDNA, optionally amplified, and sequenced in a high-throughput manner to capture a snapshot of the transcriptome profile (Figure 2B). Beyond differential gene expression analysis, RNA-seq has been employed to unveil the coordinated transcription of genes in the nucleus and mitochondria encoding for mitochondrial proteins.18,19,20,21 In a recent study, Hodgkinson and colleagues analyzed the correlation of mitochondrial and nuclear expression in the brain of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients versus healthy subjects and observed an aberrant correlation in AD patients.22 This imbalance in particular affects the synaptic and lysosomal pathways, indicating that perturbed mitochondrial turnover might have an implication in disease progression. Additionally, they showed that mitochondrial-nuclear correlation of gene expression varies between different cell types in the brain by applying single-cell RNA-seq. Along this line, it has been demonstrated that the functional consequences of mtDNA mutations are also tissue specific.23 Generally, many mtDNA mutations are harmless for the cell, allowing for a high level of mtDNA heteroplasmy without a detriment.24 Exploiting the naturally occurring variation in the mitochondrial genome as an intrinsic barcode, single-cell RNA-seq can be performed for cell lineage tracing and to study the cellular dynamics in human subjects in both physiological and pathological contexts.24,25 ‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬
The entire mitochondrial genome is transcribed as one polycistronic precursor RNA, which undergoes subsequent processing and maturation.26 Among the regulatory mechanisms of mitochondrial RNA processing, modification of mitochondrial tRNAs is pivotal and often perturbed in diseases.27,28 RNA-seq-based approaches have been used to identify mitochondrial tRNA modifications, such as methylation, by leveraging error-induced dNTP mis-incorporation or termination at the modified tRNA site during reverse transcription.27 Alternatively, approaches combining crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP), such as methylation-individual nucleotide resolution CLIP (miCLIP) or 6mA-crosslinking-exonuclease sequencing (6mACE-seq), can be applied to interrogate mtDNA modifications and epitranscriptomics.29,30 These techniques pinpoint modified sites at nucleotide resolution using antibodies against specific modification types. Utilizing miCLIP, it has been shown that mitochondrial nucleic acid modifications shape cellular metabolic changes in diseases.30
High-throughput sequencing-based methods have also been concatenated with other experimental setups to address more specific aspects of mitochondrial biology. For instance, APEX-seq identifies RNA molecules in a spatially restricted manner by combining RNA-seq with APEX-mediated proximity labeling (PL) (see also section “spatial proteomics to examine localization and interactions of mitochondrial proteins”).31 For this, a protein of interest is genetically tagged with the peroxidase APEX, which enables covalent labeling of adjacent RNA molecules with biotin. Enrichment of biotinylated RNAs is then followed by high-throughput sequencing. Notably, APEX-seq bypasses the need for conventional subcellular fractionation required to characterize the RNA pool of a specific organelle. Thus, APEX-seq is particularly advantageous for spatial sequencing of compartments for which conventional purification is challenging, such as mitochondrial sub-compartments. Fazal et al. exemplified the use of APEX-seq by fusing APEX to the cytosolic side of an outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) protein and observed that mRNAs encoding mitochondrial proteins are significantly enriched near mitochondria, presumably promoting local protein translation.31 Furthermore, they uncovered that association of these mRNAs with the OMM occurs by two distinct mechanisms—in a ribosome-independent fashion, where OMM association depends on intrinsic RNA-specific features, and in a ribosome-dependent manner, where local RNA accumulation is mediated by specific amino acid features of the corresponding emerging nascent chain during translation.
Systematic approaches to monitor translation, import, and half-lives of mitochondrial proteins
Translatomics
The total copy number of proteins in a cell is defined by the mRNA translation and protein degradation. Translation at the ribosome is highly regulated, and the abundance of newly synthesized polypeptides does not necessarily correlate with the amount of the corresponding mRNA.32 Regarding protein synthesis, mitochondria are particularly interesting and unique organelles, since they possess their own expression machinery to synthesize proteins encoded in their genome. Although the mitochondrial genome encodes for only 13 proteins, aberrant mitochondrial translation is detrimental and is associated with a number of mitochondrial diseases.33 Additionally, the partitioned location of mitochondrial genes in the mitochondrial and nuclear genome requires cross-compartment coordination of protein synthesis in the mitochondrial matrix with cytosolic translation and import of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins to ensure the assembly of functional OXPHOS complexes.
Despite the pathological relevance of proper translation of mitochondrial proteins, studies aiming at globally unveiling translatome changes of the mitochondrial proteome in disease-relevant conditions remain scarce. This can be attributed to the fact that adequate techniques for comprehensive translatome analyses emerged only recently. Currently, sequencing- and MS-based techniques are available to monitor protein translation on a global scale. The sequencing-based ribosomal profiling technique identifies and quantifies ribosome occupancy on mRNAs as a readout for translation and was first established for cytosolic ribosomes.34 For this method, polysomes extracted from cell lysates are treated with RNase to digest accessible mRNAs, leaving behind short mRNA fragments that were part of ribosomal complexes and thereby protected from digestion (Figure 2B). The obtained pool of these ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs) is then analyzed by NGS. Conventional ribosomal profiling protocols typically show a lower coverage for mitochondrial RPFs, which resulted in the development of more advanced protocols designed for improved purification of mitochondrial ribosomes (mitoribosomes) and detection of mitochondrial RPFs in order to investigate mitochondrial translation.35,36,37,38 This can be achieved by different measures, such as genetically tagging a mitoribosomal subunit and subsequent immunoprecipitation (IP), or optimization of the sucrose gradient in order to enrich for mitoribosomes. Additionally, mitochondrial RPFs show a larger size distribution compared with cytosolic ones, which can be accounted for by adjusting the RPF cutoff during analysis.37
Recently, a combinatorial approach of RNA-seq and ribosomal profiling on skeletal muscle biopsies of human subjects addressed how transcription and translation contribute to age-related decline of mitochondrial mass and revealed that 13 transcripts of mitochondrial proteins showed significantly reduced translation efficiency with age.39 Surprisingly, seven of these transcripts originated from OXPHOS genes encoded in the mitochondrial genome, pointing toward a role of compromised mitochondrial translation in age-related decline of mitochondrial proteins in skeletal muscle. Another recent study compared translation of nuclear- and mitochondria-encoded OXPHOS subunits in five different cell types and revealed balanced synthesis levels of OXPHOS proteins in the cytosol and mitochondria.35 Intriguingly, chronic mitochondrial translation deficiency did not affect translation in the cytosol but instead resulted in the activation of proteostasis pathways, indicating that translation of nuclear-encoded OXPHOS subunits is not tuned down in response to imbalanced subunit abundance. Recently, an alternative approach for isolating RPFs has been developed that omits the initial step of ribosomal purification by ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), or IP.40 Here, RPFs are generated by adding excessive amounts of RNase directly to cell lysates, followed by RNA isolation and sequencing. Since translating ribosomes protect RNA more efficiently against enzymatic digestion than other ribosomal binding proteins or scanning ribosomes, ribosomal footprints are retained on RNase treatment. This RNase footprinting approach was shown to require only very low amounts of input (as few as 1,000 cells) and is able to capture cytosolic and mitochondrial translation simultaneously.
MS-based proteomics provides an alternative approach for translatome analysis by selective labeling and monitoring of newly synthesized proteins. Several strategies using different labeling methods have been developed to mark nascent polypeptides, including bio-orthogonal/quantitative non-canonical amino acid tagging (BONCAT/QuaNCAT, labeling with a non-natural amino acid), puromycin-associated nascent chain proteomics (PUNCH-P, labeling using puromycin), and pulsed stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC, labeling using amino acids with heavy isotopes).41,42,43 For pulsed-SILAC approaches, cultured cells are supplied with heavy labeled amino acids for a limited amount of time to allow incorporation into newly translated proteins before cell harvest and sample processing. This approach was used to examine the impact of protein misfolding in the mitochondrial matrix on mitochondrial translation and revealed that mitochondrial proteotoxic stress leads to translation inhibition.44 Importantly, SILAC-labeled proteins are not enriched from the lysate, and both labeled and unlabeled peptides are analyzed by MS. This allows us not only to quantify the amount of nascent polypeptides but also to quantify the total protein abundance (the sum of labeled and unlabeled peptides). The latter can be used for sample normalization, which is particularly valuable for scenarios of global translational repression, as observed upon mitochondrial dysfunction or protein folding stress in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER).45,46,47,48
However, simultaneous quantification of the total protein abundance also harbors technical difficulties, since newly synthesized polypeptides constitute only a minor fraction of the total protein, making their accurate quantification challenging.49 This technical limitation can be overcome by applying multiplexed enhanced protein dynamics (mePROD), which was initially established to monitor the whole-cell translatome.45 This pulsed-SILAC-based approach makes use of a fully SILAC labeled so-called booster channel that is added to the sample setup to selectively amplify the signal of SILAC-labeled peptides during MS analysis (Figure 2C). The increased sensitivity allows for accurate quantification after SILAC pulses as short as 15 min, which makes it possible to examine acute translatome responses under various conditions. Importantly, the composition of the booster channel is adjustable. In the initial mePROD setup, the booster channel was derived from a whole-cell lysate to amplify the signal of the entire cellular translatome.45 However, booster channels may also be obtained from purified subcellular fractions to specifically increase identification and quantification of newly synthesized polypeptides of the particular fraction, as we showed for mitochondrial proteins.48 Using this setup, we examined the consequences of acute mitochondrial membrane depolarization on the translatome of mitochondrial proteins and observed a global translation attenuation, which was dependent on the integrated stress response.
Importomics
Mitochondrial proteins that are encoded in the nuclear genome and translated in the cytosol require a mitochondrial import. Similar to translatomics, studies aiming at characterizing the global mitochondrial importome are scarce, due to the limited repertoire of suitable techniques. We recently showed that the mePROD method can be altered to quantify mitochondrial protein import by proteomics whereby newly synthesized and pulsed-SILAC labeled proteins within mitochondria are quantified by combination of the mePROD setup with mitochondrial purification (mePRODmt).48 mePRODmt was used to analyze the effects of mitochondrial dysfunction, such as membrane depolarization and proteotoxic stress, on the mitochondrial importome.48,50 These studies revealed that mitochondrial import is strongly affected on mitochondrial malfunction and that the mitochondrial importome is defined by two factors, protein import efficiency and translation efficiency; both of which can become subject to manipulation on mitochondrial dysfunction.
Adapting mePROD approaches to ex vivo tissue samples and animal models will be a major future milestone to enable translatome and importome analyses in disease-related in situ contexts. This will require pulsed-SILAC labeling of ex vivo tissue under cell culture conditions or short-term labeling of animals by using a SILAC diet. Prolonged dietary SILAC labeling over several generations was already established and shown to be harmless for mice and flies, and standardized SILAC diets are commercially available, suggesting that in vivo mePROD might be applicable in the near future.51,52,53
Half-life measurements
MS analysis on extended SILAC labeling, i.e., up to several days, can be used to determine protein half-lives (the time required to replace half of the protein pool) and turnover rates (the rate at which proteins are replaced).54 For this, the heavy-to-total ratio (giving the fraction of “young” protein) or light-to-total ratio (giving the fraction of “old” protein) is determined after multiple periods of SILAC labeling.48,55,56 A recent study conducted in myotubes and neurons differentiated from human embryonic stem cells found that mitochondrial proteins show slower turnover than proteins of other organelles, with average half-lives of 5.5 and 7 days in myotubes and neurons, respectively.56 Notably, the protein turnover differs based on the sub-mitochondrial localization. Although OMM proteins show a similar turnover to the whole-cell average, proteins located in the intermembrane space (IMS), inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), or matrix are longer lived, particularly proteins of the electron transport chain. Another study conducted in mice of different ages characterized age-related alterations in mitochondrial protein abundance and half-lives in different muscle types.57 This approach showed that half-lives increase with age for most mitochondrial proteins. Future systematic analyses of protein turnover in the context of different mitochondrial dysfunctions would decipher the roles and contributions of both protein translation and degradation in adapting total protein abundance and function to changing mitochondrial conditions.54
PTM profiling of mitochondrial proteins
A large number of different PTMs have been characterized in mitochondria, including, but not restricted to, phosphorylation, acetylation, acylation (including succinylation, glutarylation, and malonylation), nitrosylation, sulfhydration, O-GlcNacylation, ADP-ribosylation, and deamination.58,59,60,61,62,63,64,65,66,67,68,69 Rapid adaptation of protein function and interaction is often mediated by activating or repressing PTMs rather than modulating protein abundance.70 As most PTMs are reversible, this system can be exploited for precise and fast-acting control of cellular events by an intricate interplay of enzymes promoting PTM conjugation and removal.64 As a central metabolic hub, mitochondria possess a plethora of PTMs to adjust the cellular metabolic activity to need, and changes in the mitochondrial PTM landscape were shown to be associated with aging and tumorigenesis.71,72,73,74
Due to their dynamic nature and typically low abundance, profiling PTMs possesses technical hurdles and requires additional steps, such as PTM enrichment and typically sample fractionation, before MS analysis (Figure 2C).75,76,77 PTM profiling of mitochondrial proteins does not require mitochondrial isolation per se. However, the inclusion of an additional purification step might bring about certain advantages, e.g., to prevent potential post-lysis artefacts introduced by non-mitochondrial enzymes acquiring access to mitochondrial proteins in the lysate and changing their PTM status. Depending on the PTM type, different approaches are employed to enrich PTM-modified peptides, including ion exchange chromatography (IEC), immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC), and immunoaffinity chromatography.75 The use of conventional collision-based peptide precursor ion fragmentation strategies during MS analysis can result in insufficient preservation of labile PTMs during the MS run, which led to the development of alternative methods based on electron transfer induced fragmentation.78,79,80,81 PTM analysis can be performed label free or in combination with multiplexing strategies by isobaric labeling, such as tandem mass tag (TMT), for phenotypic comparison across multiple conditions.75 In addition, PTM profiling benefited from the establishment and further improvements of data-independent acquisition (DIA).82,83 In comparison to data-dependent acquisition (DDA), where MS data are only acquired for a predefined and limited set of precursor ions, DIA collects data for all identified precursor ions, improving the sensitivity and depth of MS analysis. Once modified sites were identified, this information can be used as a base to perform functional assays to characterize the effects of such modifications. For instance, metabolomics and interactomics approaches (see sections below) can be employed in conjunction with PTM profiling to determine the changes in protein activity and interaction, respectively.
Phosphorylation was one of the first-described PTMs to regulate the activity of numerous mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, including members of the OXPHOS and the pyruvate dehydrogenase complexes.64,84 A phospho-proteomics study confirmed that approximately 90% of mitochondrial proteins harbor at least one phosphorylation site; however, the biological function remains yet unknown for most of these phosphorylation events.63 Multiple studies aiming at characterizing the mitochondrial acetylome and acylome revealed that lysine acetylation and acylation are ubiquitous PTMs.65,85 Similar to phosphorylation, acetylation and acylation are considered rapid systems to reprogram mitochondrial metabolism, and numerous metabolic enzymes were identified as targets of the deacetylase sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) and the deacylase sirtuin 5 (SIRT5).64,86,87,88,89
A topic that has gained recent interest is to elucidate how mitochondrial PTMs are connected to muscle exercise. Although these studies remain technically challenging, e.g., due to the limited availability of patient samples for comparative analyses and the high abundance of a small number of myofibrillar and metabolic proteins, existing studies identified numerous acute PTM changes in muscle cells on exercise.59,90,91,92 Several mitochondrial protein modifications have been implicated in pathologies. For instance, decreased SIRT3 expression, which is observed in obese patients and causes hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins, is associated with heart failure.93 Moreover, altered PTM profiles of components of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore complex were described in the context of cardiac diseases, and certain PTMs in cytochrome c are associated with cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer.94,95
Complexome profiling of mitochondrial proteins
Most cellular proteins fulfill their function as part of the stable or transient protein complexes. Hence, adjustment of cellular functions to need can not only be achieved on a protein level but also by modulating the complex assembly and activity. The collective set of protein complexes in a cell or particular organelle is referred to as the complexome. The OXPHOS machinery in mitochondria has been a longstanding subject of structural and functional investigation. It mediates the essential function of ATP production and requires the interplay of the respiratory chain (consisting of complexes I–IV) and the F1F0-ATP synthase (complex V).96,97,98 Important details about the composition and assembly of these complexes were gained by the MS-based complexome profiling technique.99,100,101,102,103,104,105 Complexome profiling combines fractionation of protein complexes with MS-based protein quantification (Figure 2C).106 Importantly, protein complexes have to remain in their native state throughout cell or organelle homogenization, which puts a high relevance on optimization of lysis conditions. Profiling of mitochondrial complexes would benefit from a mitochondrial purification step before homogenization, since it might help circumvent post-lysis artefacts, i.e., the formation of non-physiological complexes between proteins located in different cellular compartments. Complexes in the homogenate are then separated by size, which is achieved by various methods, such as native electrophoresis, SEC, or density gradient centrifugation (DGC). Besides providing new mechanistic insights on complex assembly under steady-state conditions, complexome profiling has been successfully applied to examine complex alterations because of various mutations related to mitochondrial diseases.107,108,109,110 For example, complexome analysis of patients suffering from Barth syndrome, who show altered lipid composition of the IMM, revealed numerous changes of the complexome profile that, surprisingly, were not restricted to the complexes located at the IMM.111 This underlines the power of complexome profiling in revealing the cause of clinical phenotypes that are not directly linked to disease-related genetic aberrations.
Spatial proteomics to examine localization and interactions of mitochondrial proteins
Local proteome mapping
Systematic mapping of the subcellular localization of the human proteome by fluorescence microscopy revealed that approximately 50% of all cellular proteins localize to more than one cellular compartment and that localization can be dynamic.112 Mitochondria are particularly interesting in spatial terms because the two mitochondrial membranes make up four distinct sub-compartments—the OMM, IMS, IMM, and matrix—which all accommodate proteins that are translocated by a series of protein translocation machineries.113 Localization changes of a particular mitochondrial protein on different stimuli or stresses may indicate important functions, e.g., in signaling. For instance, the cleavage-induced release of the mitochondrial protein DAP3 binding cell death enhancer 1 (DELE1) to the cytosol signals mitochondrial perturbation and activates a C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP)-mediated stress response.114,115 Generally, systematic evaluation of protein localizations and their dynamic changes on stimuli or in disease contexts remains scarce, which is particularly striking considering that the mutation-driven mislocalization of a single protein can lead to development of disease.116 One technique to systematically monitor the spatial distribution of proteins in cells is the dynamic organellar maps approach.117 Here, cellular compartments of a lysate are separated by biochemical fractionation and then sequentially analyzed by quantitative proteomics, which also allows us to assess the dynamic localization changes on stimuli. However, the separation of sub-organellar compartments of mitochondria by the means of biochemical fractionation is challenging. In a more recent effort, a sub-organellar spatial resolution of the mitochondrial proteome was obtained by PL for determining the local proteomes of the IMS, IMM, and matrix.118,119 In recent years, PL has become a popular approach, and multiple tools, such as employing the peroxidase APEX or the biotin ligase in proximity-dependent biotin identification (BioID), have been developed and continuously optimized to address various aspects of spatial proteomics, including protein localization, interactions, and complex formation.120,121 Although the biochemistry behind these two enzymatic systems is different, they both result in the conjugation of biotin to proximal biomolecules in a spatially restricted manner (Figure 2C). For mitochondrial localization studies, the PL enzyme is targeted to a specific sub-compartment via known targeting sequences. Spatial profiling with PL does not require conventional biochemical purification of mitochondria, such as DGC. However, an additional purification step might be considered, e.g., to reduce background derived from enzymatic activity before the tagged protein reaches the targeted compartment. Split-BioID, a further development of BioID, can circumvent this issue by splitting the active enzyme into two fragments that only assemble into a functional enzyme once the tagged proteins come in close proximity at the targeted compartment. This approach was successfully applied to define the local proteome at mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs) by fusing the two split-BioID constructs to proteins resident on the ER and OMM.122
Interactomics
Information about the composition of protein complexes and their dynamic changes can be obtained by interactome analyses. Two main omics approaches are commonly used: affinity purification of proteins of interest and their interaction partners or PL of proteins in close distance to the protein of interest (referred to as “bait”). Since biotin is covalently attached, labeled proteins can be enriched under stringent conditions, which makes PL a powerful approach to examine even transient and low-affinity interactions. A systematic study used BioID to create a high-resolution mitochondrial interaction network under steady-state conditions by performing PL experiments with 100 different mitochondrial baits, which identified more than 15,000 interactions with more than 1,400 proteins (referred to as “preys”).123 Among other findings, this study discovered a series of potential novel mitochondrial proteins and revealed hitherto unknown dual sub-organellar localizations for some mitochondrial proteins, which were not identifiable by fluorescence microscopy, highlighting the remarkable sensitivity of PL-based spatial proteomics.
PL approaches have also been successfully applied to in vivo models, such as fruit fly, C. elegans, and mice, making them a promising tool for future interactome and spatial studies in differentiated tissues and disease models.120 The recent development of spatial proteomics assays also opens up new possibilities to define further if and how mitochondrial functions are segregated within sub-compartments such as the IMS and matrix. This is particularly interesting, given the growing evidence of membraneless organelles that are formed via liquid-liquid phase separation.124 For instance, it was recently shown that mitochondrial RNA granules in the matrix possess several properties of phase-separated condensates and that mitochondrial nucleoids form by phase separation.125,126
The inherent compatibility of different MS-based proteomics methods allows us to combine multiple approaches, which could be used to characterize the spatial aspect of mitochondrial functions, e.g., if protein modification or mitochondrial translation is restricted to specific microenvironments within mitochondrial sub-compartments.
N-terminomics to define substrate spectra of mitochondrial proteases
Mitochondria host sets of proteins that maintain protein homeostasis (proteostasis). Among them are a highly conserved group of mitoproteases, which are pivotal for both the processing of newly synthesized mitochondrial proteins and the removal of damaged or misfolded components.127,128 There are at least 23 mitochondria-exclusive proteases in humans, which are located in different sub-mitochondrial compartments.127 However, despite the significance of mitoproteases for organismal health, the identities of their target proteins remain largely unknown.128
One approach to identify novel mitoprotease substrates aims to monitor peptide fragments that are generated by proteolytic cleavage. This cleavage will produce new N termini and C termini in substrates, which can serve as footprints for protease activity during MS analysis. The technical challenge of this setup is that MS-based proteomic analysis generally requires protein fragmentation, which is typically achieved by tryptic digestion in a so-called bottom-up approach.129 Thus, N-terminomics requires special measures to be able to differentiate fragments generated by the mitoprotease from those generated by tryptic digestion later during MS sample preparation. Several techniques were specifically developed for this purpose, including combined fractional diagonal chromatography (COFRADIC),130 subtiligase-131 and chemical-based enrichment of protease substrates (CHOPSs),132 and terminal amine isotopic labeling of substrates (TAILSs).133 Commonly, these techniques first preserve the native and neo N-terminal footprints of substrates resulting from protease activity, which can be achieved either by blocking or introducing a tag/protein to the N termini, followed by tryptic digestion for MS sample preparation (Figure 2C).134 The following step aims to enrich for N-terminal peptides by separating them from internal peptides resulting from tryptic digestion. Positive selection, such as IP, can be used to isolate the N-terminal peptides if tags were introduced.131,132 Alternatively, negative selection can be applied to trap and thus deplete internal peptides and elute previously blocked N-terminal peptides.130,133 Finally, the enriched N-terminal peptides are recovered and identified via MS analysis.134 N-terminomics has been used to identify substrates of several mitochondrial proteases, including Clp-XP and HtrA2/Omi.135,136
Beyond their role in protein quality control, mitoproteases influence the mitochondrial function by adjusting the levels of regulatory mitochondrial proteins.127,128 Indeed, mitoproteases were shown to play a role in diverse mitochondrial processes, including mitochondrial gene expression, protein import and maturation, protein complex assembly, phospholipid metabolism, mitochondrial dynamics, and cell death regulation.127 Therefore, mitochondrial N-terminomics could be used to detect regulatory activities in mitochondria and to evaluate the overall mitochondrial functionality under different physiological conditions. Indeed, mitochondrial N-terminomics has been recently employed to capture changes in mitochondrial proteolysis linked to mitochondrial dysfunction in a cellular model of Parkinson’s disease.134 Given the central role of mitoproteases in maintaining mitochondrial proteostasis and function, it will be critical to develop further tools to gain a better understanding of the spatio-temporal role of mitoproteases regarding their broad sets of substrates.
Metabolomics to characterize the metabolic state of mitochondria
Mitochondria play a critical role as metabolic hubs, which provide most ATPs for the cell through aerobic respiration and host a plethora of metabolic processes to produce building blocks for macromolecules such as lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.137 Beyond these processes, mitochondria are also the source for metabolite byproducts that function as cellular cues to monitor cell homeostasis, including ROS and TCA metabolites.137,138 Metabolic processes in the mitochondria are highly dynamic, which is necessary to respond to different cellular environments, adapt to various stresses, and initiate signaling pathways.137,138
Comprehensive profiling of metabolites in a given biological system is referred to as metabolomics.139 Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and MS are the most common detection platforms for metabolomics.139,140,141 The latter shows higher sensitivity and high metabolite coverage and has thus been used more widely in the field. To further increase the sensitivity for metabolite identification, metabolites in the sample are typically separated before detection, for example, by gas chromatography (GC), liquid chromatography (LC), or capillary electrophoresis (CE) (Figure 2D).142 Each combination of analytical platforms used in metabolomics possesses its own advantages and disadvantages and will influence the types of detected metabolites.142 Both targeted and untargeted approaches, whereby specific or a broad set of metabolites are analyzed, are utilized in metabolomics.
Investigating mitochondrial metabolomics requires an initial purification step to specifically enrich mitochondria. The common purification method utilizing differential centrifugation followed by DGC yields mitochondria with high purity.143,144 However, the time-consuming protocol might result in a metabolite loss of certain enzymatic reactions. Additionally, components of the isolation buffer, such as sucrose, might interfere with metabolite detection.144,145 Alternative methods utilizing IP to directly or indirectly target OMM proteins by introducing tags have been utilized to isolate mitochondria for metabolomics.143,145,146 These methods speed up the mitochondrial purification step with minimal impurities when compared with centrifugation-based techniques.
Unlike genes and proteins that are subjected to epigenetic and post-translational regulation, respectively, metabolites represent the functional end products of gene expression and thus provide direct information about phenotypic alterations.140 Hence, metabolomics has been widely used for biomarker discovery to detect changes on certain physiological conditions or during the onset of diseases.147 Beyond understanding the etiology of diseases, mitochondrial metabolic changes might serve as signatures for the activation of particular cellular programs. For instance, primary stem cells shift their metabolism from predominantly glycolysis to a mainly OXPHOS-based metabolism during differentiation.148 In addition, a lipidomics study in mouse brain has shown that mitochondrial lipid composition changes during aging, where a reduced mitochondrial function is often observed.149 Changes of these metabolites in mitochondria could therefore be used as indicators of cellular alterations observed during stress responses (such as exercise), cancer, and aging.137
Concluding remarks
The omics field witnessed a dramatic surge of technological advances and novel applications throughout the past few years. The great advantage of these applications is that they are able to examine hundreds to thousands of cellular macromolecules, such as nucleic acids, proteins, or metabolites, simultaneously. Therefore, omics approaches have the power to reveal indirect consequences of mitochondrial stresses or genetic aberrations, which might help to explain mitochondrial and clinical phenotypes that are observed in these contexts but cannot be directly attributed to the mitochondrial function that is compromised by the given stressor or genetic mutation. As introduced in the sections above, various types of omics approaches have already been applied to analyze different aspects of mitochondrial changes in several stress and disease contexts. However, how alterations on these different layers (e.g., transcription, translation, import, degradation, PTMs, and complex formation) are connected and translated into functional changes still remains unclear in most cases. Therefore, current attempts aim to move away from analyzing only a single aspect of mitochondrial biology and rather bring together multiple omics methods to obtain a holistic picture of how mitochondrial performance is compromised under stress and disease conditions.21,150,151,152,153,154,155,156 For instance, an ample study conducted in mammalian cell culture analyzed how cells globally adapt on the translatome, proteome, and metabolome levels when mitochondria are subjected to different challenges, such as compromised mitochondrial translation, protein import, membrane potential, and OXPHOS.21 Among other findings, this multi-omics dataset revealed the activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) as the key regulator for mitochondrial stress-induced transcriptomic reprogramming. Ideally, multi-omics studies are conducted on the same pool of cells as input material, requiring tailored protocols for the simultaneous extraction of different macromolecules from the same sample, which has already been successfully established for parallel lipidomic, metabolomic, and proteomic profiling.157
Most recently, based on the establishment of single-cell genomics, there have been extensive efforts to develop approaches for multi-omics analyses on the single-cell level.158,159 Single-cell multi-omics aims to resolve intercellular heterogeneity within a cell population on genome, epigenome, transcriptome, and proteome levels, the combination of which determines the identity of a cell.159 Some of these approaches, i.e., PHAGE-ATAC and DOGMA-seq, include sequencing of mtDNA as natural barcodes for clonal tracing and represent promising tools to systematically elaborate the impact of mtDNA mutations on protein expression.160,161 PHAGE-assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) uses recombinant phage display to simultaneously monitor chromatin accessibility, mtDNA mutations, and intracellular and cell surface protein abundance, whereas proteome analysis of DOGMA-seq is limited to cell surface proteins; however, it additionally generates transcriptome data. Although MS-based approaches for comprehensive proteome and PTM profiling are established on a single-cell level, they are not compatible with established single-cell multi-omics protocols yet, as they would require parallel extraction of nucleic acids and proteins from the same cell.159,162,163,164,165,166,167 Instead, proteome analysis in single-cell multi-omics setups currently involves the utilization of a set of antibodies conjugated with single-stranded DNA, targeting different epitopes on a protein of interest.168,169 On binding to the protein, the antibodies co-localize and enable ligation and generation of a DNA sequence that is detectable by qPCR. Inevitably, this approach highly limits the number of traceable proteins in single-cell multi-omics setups to date. Future efforts will be required to further improve the robustness and sensitivity of existing single-cell multi-omics approaches and to integrate other powerful omics approaches, such as MS-based proteomics and metabolomics.159 Undoubtedly, single-cell multi-omics has great potential to resolve exciting questions in mitochondrial biology, e.g., the impact of mtDNA mutations and heteroplasmy on the single-cell level or to unveil cellular variations within tissues in mitochondrial disease contexts. However, as discussed in the previous sections and summarized in Figure 2A, some omics approaches benefit or even rely on mitochondrial purification. Thus, implementing high-throughput mitochondrial isolation from single cells will be another great milestone toward establishing single-cell analyses by multi-omics in the context of mitochondrial biology.
Besides the technical obstacles that still need to be overcome to establish multi-omics and single-cell multi-omics as routine applications in the mitochondrial context, integration and analysis of the large data amounts generated in multi-omics studies also still possess certain challenges.170,171 Nevertheless, systematic multi-omics analyses—on population, and ultimately, single-cell level—of different kinds of mitochondrial dysfunction have the power to not only provide a holistic network of how different aspects of mitochondrial biology are interconnected but also eventually could serve as a diagnostic tool to connect pathological phenotypes to their molecular origin.
